\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Critical recording requirements}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The 60\%-weighted criterion asks for \emph{``capturing ArUco marker images, providing clear visual proof''}. A terminal banner is not an image. Show the \textbf{camera feed} with the detected marker outlined and its ID drawn on it (\texttt{cv2.aruco.drawDetectedMarkers}).
  \item \textbf{Layout:} Gazebo left ($\sim$60\%), camera feed top-right, mission log bottom-right. 1920$\times$1080 in OBS.
  \item Scenario~3 footage at 2$\times$ with an on-screen caption saying so.
  \item The Scenario~2 and~4 baseline failures are the most valuable footage in the whole video --- they carry the argument. Show the drone driving into the obstacle and the watchdog abandoning the leg. Note the report describes strikes as \emph{inferred} from altitude traces rather than measured, so narrate what is visible on screen and do not claim instrumented collision detection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Segment 1 --- Problem and Approach (0:00--0:35)}

\cue{Gazebo wide shot of Scenario~2: drone on pad, five obstacle blocks, ArUco markers on stands}

\timecode{0:00}
``The task is to fly a drone around a set of inspection viewpoints, photograph an ArUco marker at each one, and avoid every obstacle.

\timecode{0:08}
Two decisions: what order to visit the viewpoints in, and how to route between them. Ordering is nearest-neighbour with 2-opt. For routing I compare flying straight at the target, 3D A-star on a voxel grid, and RRT-star in continuous space.

\timecode{0:20}
Rather than three fixed configurations, I ran the full grid: both orderings crossed with all three planners, three times each, on four scenarios. Seventy-two runs. That's what lets me attribute each effect to a specific component.''

\section*{Segment 2 --- Demonstration (0:35--2:20)}

\subsection*{Scenario 1 --- 10 viewpoints, 1 obstacle (0:35--0:52)}

\cue{Split-screen: file ordering left, NN+2-opt right, both with A*. Camera inset showing marker detections.}

\timecode{0:35}
``Scenario 1, ten viewpoints. Same planner both sides, only the ordering differs. On the left, file order: the path crosses itself repeatedly. On the right, 2-opt: no crossings.

\timecode{0:42}
That change alone takes the path from 159 metres down to 96, and the tour from 130 seconds to 99. Every marker confirmed on both.''

\subsection*{Scenario 2 --- 10 viewpoints, 5 obstacles (0:52--1:25)}

\cue{First 6\,s: baseline clip --- drone flying straight into an obstacle. Caption: ``BASELINE --- OBSTACLE STRIKE''. Then cut to A*.}

\timecode{0:52}
``Scenario 2 adds five obstacles, and this is where straight-line flight breaks. It flies straight at a viewpoint sitting behind an obstacle, collides, and the watchdog gives up on that leg. Across three runs it reached just over half the viewpoints. Success rate: zero.

\timecode{1:08}
A-star routes around all five blocks and completes every run --- 136 seconds, all ten markers confirmed. Note it flies further than the baseline, 156 metres against 105. It's not taking a shortcut. It's doing the whole job.''

\subsection*{Scenario 3 --- 20 viewpoints, 1 obstacle (1:25--1:52)}

\cue{Split-screen at 2$\times$, ``2$\times$ SPEED'' caption.}

\timecode{1:25}
``Scenario 3 doubles the viewpoints to twenty --- the clearest case for the tour optimiser. File order flies 226 metres, 2-opt flies 119, and the tour drops from 242 seconds to 163.

\timecode{1:40}
Every planner finishes this one. With a single obstacle in an open arena, this scenario tests ordering, not avoidance.''

\subsection*{Scenario 4 --- 5 viewpoints, 10 obstacles (1:52--2:20)}

\cue{Show baseline failing, then A* threading the obstacle field.}

\timecode{1:52}
``Scenario 4 inverts it: five viewpoints, ten obstacles. With only five viewpoints there's essentially nothing to reorder, so ordering buys nothing here. Obstacle avoidance is everything.

\timecode{2:04}
The straight-line baseline reaches two thirds of the viewpoints and never once finishes the mission. A-star completes all three runs in 73 seconds. The baseline's 77 seconds looks comparable, but it never finished, so that number doesn't mean anything. RRT-star also completes, though under file ordering it managed only two runs in three.''

\section*{Segment 3 --- Results (2:20--3:26)}

\cue{Full-screen \texttt{report\_figs/fig\_reliability.png}, then the table below}

\timecode{2:20}
``Here is the whole grid. Two findings, pointing in opposite directions.

\timecode{2:27}
Ordering drives efficiency. Holding the planner fixed, 2-opt cuts path length by 38 to 50 per cent in the first three scenarios, and cuts tour time in every comparison where both configurations actually finished --- nine out of twelve.

\timecode{2:42}
The obstacle-aware planners push efficiency the other way. A-star adds up to 40 seconds. On time alone they look worse than flying straight.

\timecode{2:52}
Now look at the success column. Straight-line flight hits zero per cent in two conditions. A-star completes every one of its twenty-four runs. The seconds it costs you are buying reliability.

\timecode{3:06}
One caution --- where success is below a hundred per cent, the times and distances aren't comparable. That 105-metre baseline quit early. Read success first.

\timecode{3:18}
A-star and RRT-star came within a metre of each other on path, so RRT-star's continuous-space advantage didn't show up at this sample budget.''

\section*{Segment 4 --- Conclusions (3:26--3:55)}

\cue{Drone landing at origin, or the four-scenario path figure}

\timecode{3:26}
``So the two components do different jobs. Ordering makes the mission efficient; obstacle-aware planning gets it finished. Tune on time alone and you end up choosing the configuration that leaves the survey incomplete.

\timecode{3:39}
A-star is the better default. Same path quality as RRT-star here, comparable time, and it's the only configuration that completed all twenty-four runs. Next would be tuning RRT-star's sample budget and adding smooth trajectory generation. Thanks for watching.''
